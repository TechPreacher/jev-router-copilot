# Recommendation: don't pivot fully to B2B yet. Run a 90-day B2B test with go/no-go gates

**Known facts:** 18 months of runway, and B2C growth has been flat for two quarters. Nothing else is known. Every target below is an illustrative threshold to set yourselves, not a claim about your business.

## Bottom line
Flat growth for two quarters is a warning, but it doesn't prove B2B is the answer. A full pivot now would spend your runway on an untested idea. Staying put risks drifting until it's too late to raise money.

The recommendation has three parts:
- **Investigate B2C:** work out why growth stopped.
- **Test B2B:** run a cheap 90-day test for real business demand.
- **Decide by month 4–5:** choose one direction then, while you still have about 12 months left to execute and raise.

## Why time is the real constraint
- **Fundraising takes about 6 months.** Starting at 6–9 months of runway is dangerous. In practice you have about 9–12 months to produce evidence investors will accept.
- **B2B sales are slow.** First paid deals often take 3–9 months. If B2B is the bet, testing has to start now.
- **Reducing spend early buys time.** Cut costs that support B2C growth before it's diagnosed (paid acquisition especially). Moving runway from 18 to 22+ months may be the cheapest risk reduction available.

## Step 0: diagnose B2C first (weeks 0–4)
"Flat" can mean very different things:
- **Retention:** do users who joined in different months stick around, or is your user base leaking? Leaking means the product isn't working well enough for consumers.
- **Acquisition:** can you get new users at a cost that pays back? Rising costs mean the channel is saturated, not that the product failed.
- **Activation:** do new users reach the point where the product proves its value?
- **Monetization:** what are revenue per user, conversion to paid, and gross margin?
- **B2B signals:** are businesses already using the product, for example people signing up with work email, teams sharing accounts, or companies asking for invoices? That is your best B2B lead.

**How to read the result:**
- **Strong retention, weak acquisition:** fix distribution before pivoting the business model. B2B is optional.
- **Weak retention:** the product isn't working for consumers. A pivot is more justified, but B2B buyers need a sharper problem, not the same product sold differently.
- **Organic business usage exists:** strongest case for B2B.

## Options and key risks

### A. Stay B2C and optimize
**Fits if:** retention is healthy and the problem is acquisition or monetization.

**Risks:**
- Two flat quarters often means the market or main channel is saturated. More tweaking may not change that.
- Investors discount B2C without growth. A Series A or bridge round gets hard.
- Consumer costs (paid ads, app store fees, platform changes) are mostly outside your control.
- **Biggest risk:** months 6–12 go to small fixes, and a pivot becomes impossible by the time you admit it isn't working.

### B. Full B2B pivot now
**Fits if:** you already have unprompted business demand (inbound requests, letters of intent, pilots) and B2C retention is weak.

**Risks:**
- **No proof of demand.** Nothing known shows businesses will pay. You'd be betting the company on an assumption.
- **Sales cycles eat runway.** Procurement, security reviews (SOC 2, data protection) and integrations push revenue out 6–12 months.
- **Skills mismatch.** B2B selling, account management and enterprise features need different people. Hiring takes time and money.
- **Product rework.** Admin tools, single sign-on, permissions, billing and reporting are expensive and slow the core product.
- **You lose your B2C base**, along with the team's knowledge and the company's story. A "pivot narrative" also makes some investors cautious.
- **Wrong customer segment.** "B2B" is broad: small businesses, mid-market and enterprise behave very differently. Picking the wrong one wastes runway.

### C. Staged test: B2C on maintenance, 90-day B2B validation (recommended)
**How to run it:**
- **Team:** put 1–2 people (a founder plus one engineer) on B2B. Everyone else keeps B2C running on low cost.
- **Pick a narrow wedge:** one type of buyer, one problem. Base it on whoever already uses or asks for the product, if anyone does.
- **Interviews:** aim for 20–30 discovery conversations with potential buyers.
- **Sell before building:** offer paid pilots or letters of intent. Don't build B2B features until a pilot is signed, beyond the minimum that pilot needs.
- **Fix B2C basics in parallel:** retention and activation only, with no new paid acquisition.

**Risks:**
- **Split focus:** neither track gets enough effort, and both results come back unclear. Counter this with a hard deadline and pre-agreed gates.
- **False positives:** friendly interviews or free pilots look like demand but aren't. Count only money, signed commitments, or named buyers with budget.
- **Burnout:** small teams can't run two businesses for long. 90 days, not 12 months.
- **Team politics:** the B2C team may see the test as a threat. Explain why you're doing it openly.

### D. Fallbacks to keep open (not primary)
- **Cut deeper to reach 24+ months of runway:** buys time but slows both tracks.
- **Acquihire or strategic sale:** worth an early, quiet conversation if gates fail. It's worth more while runway still exists.

## Decision gates at day 90 (illustrative; set your own numbers now)
| Signal | Go B2B | Stay B2C | Neither / reassess |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paid pilots or letters of intent | e.g. ≥3 paid pilots, or a qualified pipeline with budget-holders | <1 | — |
| Buyer pull | Repeated, specific pain; they ask "when can we start" | Polite interest only | — |
| B2C retention | Irrelevant if B2B is strong | Improving trend after fixes | Flat or declining |
| Sales cycle seen | Pilot-to-contract path plausible within ~6 months | — | — |
| Implied unit economics | Contract value easily covers sales cost | Customer value well above acquisition cost, with recovery under ~12 months | Neither works |

- **Go B2B:** commit fully, move B2C to maintenance or shut it down, and hire or reassign for sales.
- **Stay B2C:** double down on the channel or retention fix the diagnosis found.
- **Neither:** cut burn hard and explore a sale, or a different pivot, while about 12 months remain.

**Rule:** write the gates down before the test starts, and treat missing data as a no.

## 90-day plan
- **Days 0–30:**
  - Cut non-essential spending.
  - Run the B2C diagnosis.
  - Choose the B2B wedge and start interviews.
  - Set the gates.
- **Days 31–60:**
  - Pitch paid pilots and price them early.
  - Ship B2C retention fixes.
  - Do only the minimum product work a signed pilot needs.
- **Days 61–90:**
  - Run pilots and measure gate metrics.
  - Prepare the fundraising story for whichever path wins.
  - Decide by day 90 with no extension, or at most a single 30-day one with explicit criteria.

## What would change this recommendation
- **Pivot now** if you already have strong inbound business demand or paying business users.
- **Stay B2C** if retention is strong and flat growth is clearly a fixable acquisition-channel problem.
- **Start fundraising, sale or deep cuts immediately** if real runway is shorter than 18 months (for example, burn is rising).
